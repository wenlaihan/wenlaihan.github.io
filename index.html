I study how selectivity arises in reversible reaction networks. Using dynamic radical chemistry, I remodel the skeletons and peripheries of saturated, C(sp3)-rich molecules — and I build differentiable kinetic models so that the behavior of those networks becomes something we can measure and predict rather than only rationalize. Before MIT, I studied chemistry and computer science at Carleton College.

Research

Reaction-network control and pathway differentiation

Selectivity is conventionally traced to a single rate-determining step. In a densely connected, reversible network it can instead emerge from how many rate constants relate to one another, so that a shared intermediate is directed down one pathway rather than another by modest changes in catalyst or conditions. I am interested in what makes such branch points controllable, and in the physical-organic factors that predict which way they tip.

Skeletal and peripheral remodeling under dynamic kinetic control

Direct C–H functionalization of saturated medium-sized rings is hard to control, because so many positions are comparably reactive. In our published work on seven-membered rings, reversible hydrogen atom abstraction and donation sample radicals at multiple ring positions, and selectivity is settled downstream instead: a radical addition and β-scission sequence migrates the ketone across the ring, converting 1,1-disubstituted acylcycloheptanes into 1,4-disubstituted products under mild photocatalytic conditions. Committing late rather than early is what makes a conformationally mobile substrate tractable.

Differentiable kinetic modeling

When selectivity is set by many coupled rate constants rather than one barrier, qualitative reasoning runs out. I build kinetic models alongside the bench work: networks specified symbolically in Catalyst.jl, integrated with the stiff solvers of DifferentialEquations.jl, and regressed against time-resolved data by global optimization before refinement with adjoint sensitivities (SciMLSensitivity.jl). What draws me is the inverse problem — recovering a network from its timecourses, rather than only simulating one forward.

Metal–ligand cooperativity

As an undergraduate with Prof. Matt Whited at Carleton College, I worked on cobalt–silicon complexes in which the Co=Si bond offers two chemically distinct sites for substrate activation. Their orthogonal binding preferences let a cobalt silylene catalyze nitrene transfer from an alkyl azide to carbon monoxide while avoiding the CO poisoning that would otherwise stall turnover at a single site. A later study on the same platform found that bulky tert-butyl groups on the phosphine donors hinder reactivity at both cobalt and silicon, making the catalysis markedly less effective than with smaller isopropyl and cyclohexyl analogues.

Current work extends these ideas to new scaffolds and reaction classes.
